## Facebook Post

You can say five grammatically perfect sentences and still lose the room.

"Sales declined 12%. We lost two distributors. We are launching a promotion next quarter." Nothing a grammar teacher would flag — and your listener is still doing the work of figuring out how those three facts relate to each other.

That's the communication skill most advanced English speakers were never taught: your sentences can be correct individually and disconnected collectively. The fix isn't more vocabulary. It's two frameworks — Claim → Evidence → Explanation → Link to build the argument, and Look Back → Connect → Move Forward to build the flow.

## Twitter/X Thread

**Tweet 1 (Hook):**
"Sales declined 12%. We lost two distributors. We are launching a promotion next quarter."

Three grammatically perfect sentences. And the listener still has to figure out how the facts relate to each other.

Your grammar isn't the problem. Your sentences aren't connected. →

**Tweet 2:**
Strong English isn't a collection of correct sentences.

It's a chain of connected ideas — each sentence inherits something from the one before it and hands something to the one after.

When a link is missing, the listener does your reasoning for you. Senior listeners do that twice. Then they downgrade you.

**Tweet 3:**
Framework 1 — build the argument:

Claim → Evidence → Explanation → Link

Most professionals skip the Explanation. They say "inventory is up 18%" and never say why 18% matters. Evidence doesn't speak for itself. It never does.

**Tweet 4:**
"Furthermore" won't save you.

Connectors describe relationships. They don't create them. "Furthermore, we need to invest in sales" — if the logical link isn't there, the connector is decoration on a gap. And it sounds mechanical.

**Tweet 5:**
Framework 2 — build the flow:

Look Back → Connect → Move Forward

"We stabilized production in Q2. That solved capacity — but it exposed another constraint. Our next priority is supplier reliability."

The listener never wonders why you changed topics.

## LinkedIn Post (HOLD — pending LinkedIn Page channel decision)

You're giving the quarterly update. Every sentence is grammatically correct:

"Sales declined 12%. We lost two distributors. We are launching a promotion next quarter."

And across the table, your US counterpart is doing work you can't see — trying to figure out how those facts relate to each other. Did the distributors cause the decline? Is the promotion the answer to that, or to something else?

**You gave them correct English. You made them assemble the argument themselves.**

This is the communication gap I see constantly with senior professionals whose English tests at C1: the sentences are correct individually and disconnected collectively. No grammar teacher will ever flag it — which is exactly why nobody ever fixed it.

Two frameworks close the gap:

→ **Build the argument: Claim → Evidence → Explanation → Link.** Most professionals skip the Explanation — they state the evidence and assume it speaks for itself. It never does. "Slow-moving inventory is up 18%" means nothing until you say it's tying up working capital.

→ **Build the flow: Look Back → Connect → Move Forward.** "We stabilized production in Q2. That solved the capacity problem — but it exposed another constraint. Our next priority is supplier reliability." The listener never has to wonder why you changed topics.

And the trap to avoid: adding connectors. *Furthermore* and *moreover* describe relationships — they don't create them. A connector in front of a disconnected sentence sounds worse than no connector at all.
