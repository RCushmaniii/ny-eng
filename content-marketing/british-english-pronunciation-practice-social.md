## Facebook Post

A British colleague tells you your work is "quite good."

In American English that is praise. In British English it is a downgrade — it means acceptable, not excellent. Same two words, opposite verdicts, and nobody tells you which one you just received.

That is the half of British English nobody warns you about. The accent is the easier part, and even that changes three things at once: the R at the end of a syllable disappears, so water becomes WAW-tuh. The A in ask and after moves back and lengthens. The T in the middle of a word gets swallowed, so bottle becomes BO-uhl.

If you learned American English and a London office just joined your calls, your ear was trained on none of it.

## Twitter/X Thread

**Tweet 1 (Hook):**
A British colleague says your work is "quite good."

In American English that is praise.

In British English it means acceptable, not excellent.

Same two words. Opposite verdicts. And nobody tells you which one you just got.

**Tweet 2:**
Most advice about British English is about the accent.

The accent is the easier half.

The harder half is that British professional English routinely says the opposite of what it means, and does it politely enough that you do not notice.

**Tweet 3:**
The three sounds that change at once, if you learned American English:

- The R at the end of a syllable disappears. Water is WAW-tuh.
- The A in ask and after moves back and lengthens. AHSK, AHF-tuh.
- The T in the middle gets swallowed. Bottle is BO-uhl.

**Tweet 4:**
Notice what those three have in common.

They are all sounds your ear was trained to expect and no longer hears. You are not missing vocabulary. You are missing the signal that tells you which word was said.

That is a listening problem, not a speaking problem.

**Tweet 5:**
Which is the good news.

You do not need a British accent to work with a British team. Your own accent is fine.

What costs you in a meeting is missing a word your colleague said — never the way you said it back.

**Tweet 6:**
The phrases that carry a hidden verdict:

- "That's quite good" — acceptable, not excellent
- "That's not bad" — genuinely good
- "I'm not entirely sure I agree" — I disagree
- "With the greatest respect" — you are wrong
- "I'll bear it in mind" — nothing will happen

**Tweet 6b (optional, cut if the thread runs long):**
Notice that "quite good" and "not bad" point in opposite directions from where an American ear puts them.

"Quite" downgrades. "Not bad" upgrades. If you swap them in a performance conversation, you will walk out with exactly the wrong idea about how it went.

**Tweet 7:**
The rule of thumb: in British English, enthusiasm is downgraded and disagreement is upholstered.

When a British colleague sounds mildly positive, read it one notch lower than you would from an American. When they sound mildly hesitant, read it as no.
