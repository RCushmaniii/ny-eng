## Facebook Post (EN)

Students ask me every week: what should I do to improve my English?

Read more? Watch Netflix in English? Podcasts? Duolingo?

Yes — do all of it if you enjoy it. But none of those is speaking, and speaking is a skill. You'd never expect to get good at tennis by watching tennis.

Ten minutes a day beats three hours on Sunday. And the fastest change I can give anyone: stop collecting words, start collecting patterns. Not "depend" — "depend on." Not "talk" — "talk to someone."

What's one English phrase you use so often you never have to think about it?

## Twitter/X Thread

**Tweet 1 (Hook):**
The question I get most from my English students:

"What should I do to improve my English?"

Read more? Netflix? Podcasts? Duolingo?

Yes. Do all of it. But none of that is speaking — and speaking is a skill. →

**Tweet 2:**
You'd never expect to become a good tennis player by watching tennis videos.

You'd never expect to become a great presenter by reading books about presentations and never standing in front of anyone.

English works the same way. Input builds knowledge. Output builds fluency.

**Tweet 3:**
Stop collecting words. Start collecting patterns.

Knowing "depend" is useful. Knowing "depend on" is far more useful.

"Our success depends on customer service."
"It depends on the price."

Two patterns a week. Not twenty. Two. Say them out loud until they're yours.

**Tweet 4:**
This is also how you stop translating in your head.

If you build "depende de" from two separate translations every time, that's two lookups and a guess, mid-sentence.

Once "it depends on" is one unit, you don't assemble it. You retrieve it.

**Tweet 5:**
And don't only practice comfortable English.

A client pushes back on price. Someone asks the question you didn't prepare for. An executive interrupts your presentation.

Ask your partner — or your coach — to interrupt you and change direction without warning. The stress is deliberate.
