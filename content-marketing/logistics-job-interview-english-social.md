## Twitter/X Thread

**Tweet 1 (Hook):**
"We had some problems with the shipments and I helped to fix them."

That sentence is true.

It is also the sentence of an assistant — said by someone who has run a cross-border operation for six years, the moment the interview switches to English.

**Tweet 2:**
Here is what is actually happening.

Under interview pressure, non-native speakers fall back to the safest grammar they own. The past continuous and the vague plural.

We were having issues. There were some delays. They had problems with the carrier.

**Tweet 3:**
It is safe. It is also invisible.

Notice what is missing: you. No decision, no number, no consequence.

In an operations role, that absence is the whole interview.

**Tweet 4:**
Read a logistics job description and you see twenty bullet points.

Sit in the interview and there are three questions, wearing different clothes:

- Can you hold the line when the operation breaks?
- Do you own a number, or just report one?
- Can you move people who don't report to you?

**Tweet 5:**
"Tell me about a delayed shipment" is question one.
"Walk me through your OTIF" is question two.
"How do you handle a difficult carrier" is question three.

Once you hear the real question underneath, you stop telling stories and start giving evidence.

**Tweet 6:**
The structure that survives pressure. Four beats, fifteen seconds:

Situation in one line, then the number, then what you decided, then what it cost or saved.

It is specific enough that you cannot hide inside it. That is the point.

**Tweet 7:**
Before:
"We were having problems with a shipment and I helped to solve the situation."

After:
"A customs hold put forty containers three days behind on our Laredo lane. I moved the priority SKUs to an expedited carrier and held the rest."

A lane. A number. A decision.
