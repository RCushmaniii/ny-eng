## The credential block

Paste-ready. **Claims-policy compliant** — no aggregate career-year count, no
Praxair/Kodak fusion, "bilingual" never applied to Robert personally.

```
Robert Cushman III is an executive English coach in Guadalajara, Mexico, working one-on-one with directors, founders and C-suite leaders at companies including Driscoll's, CEVA Logistics and Teramar Brands. Before coaching, he spent his career in Fortune 500 IT leadership, running global technology initiatives across a 26,000-employee, 42-country organization. He is a native English speaker who now trains Spanish-speaking executives to lead in English rather than merely survive in it.
```

Short variant for tight bio fields:

```
Robert Cushman III is an executive English coach in Guadalajara, Mexico, and a former Fortune 500 IT leader. He coaches directors and founders at companies including Driscoll's and CEVA Logistics.
```

**Never write, in any pitch:** any total career-year figure ("17 years", "20 years"),
"bilingual" as a personal attribute, "trained on", or a client outcome statistic that
is not in a real client record.

## The four angles that make him quotable

Journalists do not want an English teacher. They want a named practitioner with a
non-obvious view. These are the beats where he is genuinely the right source.

**1. Nearshoring and the Mexico–US talent pipeline.**
Companies moving operations to Mexico discover their managers can pass an English test
and still cannot run a cross-border meeting. He sees the failure mode weekly. Strong
for: business press, logistics trade, HR and talent publications.

**2. Remote and hybrid work as a language problem.**
Video calls remove every cue non-native speakers rely on — no room read, no side
conversation, latency that punishes hesitation. A person who is fluent in person can
be functionally silent on Zoom. Strong for: future-of-work, management, remote-work
publications.

**3. The competence–perception gap.**
His clients are not bad at English. They are being *perceived* as less senior than
they are because their English register sounds like a student rather than a director.
That is a leadership and promotion story, not a language story. Strong for: career
advice, leadership, DEI-adjacent coverage.

**4. Why corporate language training fails.**
Group academies teach hotel-booking vocabulary to people who negotiate contracts.
Companies buy seats, measure attendance, and never measure whether anyone can now
defend a budget. Strong for: L&D, HR tech, corporate training coverage.

## Answer template

Featured and HARO answers are used near-verbatim. Write the quote, not a pitch.

```
[One-sentence direct answer to the question asked. No preamble, no "great question."]

[Two to four sentences of specific mechanism or example. A concrete scene beats an adjective: what happens in the meeting, what the person did, what changed.]

[One sentence of contrarian or non-obvious framing, if the answer has one.]
```

Rules that decide whether it gets used:

- **Answer the question that was asked.** The most common rejection is a source
  answering the question they wished had been asked.
- **150 words maximum** unless the request says otherwise. Editors cut long answers
  rather than trim them.
- **One concrete scene** — a real situation, anonymized. "A logistics director who
  could present fine but froze the moment a US counterpart interrupted him" is usable;
  "many clients struggle with confidence" is not.
- **No links in the body.** The bio field carries the link; a link in the answer reads
  as promotion and gets the whole submission dropped.
- **Never invent a client, a number, or an outcome.** If it is not in a real client
  record, it does not exist.
