You passed the level where English was the problem a long time ago. You run meetings in it. You read contracts in it. Nobody would call you a learner.

And still, there are moments when you can feel your English shrink.

Someone turns to you and says, "What do you think?" with no warning. A colleague pushes back hard and you either go quiet or come out sharper than you meant. The meeting ends, the talk turns to the election or somebody's kid's school district, and you are suddenly a spectator. You write an email that is correct in every line and somehow twice as long as it should be.

None of that is a grammar problem. More grammar will not fix it.

## The plateau has a shape

Here is what I see in almost every senior professional I coach: the English that got them to fluency is not the English that is holding them back.

**Fluent professionals don't have a level problem. They have a situation problem.**

Your English is strong in the situations you have rehearsed for years: the status update, the client call, the technical explanation. It gets thin in the situations nobody ever taught you, because no course covers them. General English courses stop at "advanced," and advanced means grammar and vocabulary. It does not mean holding a room, or disagreeing with a VP without damage, or knowing what to say when the conversation turns to church.

So I built five free courses, one for each situation where I watch fluent people stall. They are on the site now, in English and in Spanish. No account, no email. Your progress saves in your browser.

## 1. When you have to persuade a room

Most professionals prepare a presentation by preparing slides. Then they stand up and read them.

[Speak to Persuade](/en/course/persuasive-speaking/) is ten lessons built on the oldest framework in public speaking, and still the best one: credibility, logic and emotion. You learn how to earn the right to be listened to in the first thirty seconds, how to build an argument a skeptical listener can follow, and how to make people care, which is the part analytical speakers skip.

Every lesson ends with a timed exercise you do out loud. Reading about speaking does not make anyone a better speaker.

## 2. When someone disagrees with you

In a second language, conflict pushes people to one of two bad places. You stay silent and resent it, or you say it plainly and it lands harder than you intended. In Spanish you have a dozen ways to soften a no. In English you were never given them.

[Disagree Without Damage](/en/course/productive-conflict/) is ten lessons on doing it properly: lowering the temperature, disagreeing with the idea and not the person, asking a question where you were about to make an accusation, and repairing things afterward. The goal is not to avoid the disagreement. A disagreement handled well is one of the few moments when people actually change their minds.

## 3. When you have to write

Your writing is correct. That is the problem with it: correct is where most professional writing stops.

[Writing Better](/en/course/business-writing/) starts with the technical layer, the sentence-boundary and grammar errors that quietly cost credibility, and then moves to the layer that separates a competent email from one that gets acted on. Put the point first. Cut what the reader does not need. Choose the verb that does the work. Each lesson gives you a weak version to rewrite before you see mine.

## 4. When the meeting ends

This is the one nobody admits to. Your English holds for an hour of business, and then it is five minutes of small talk that does the damage.

The [Conversation Briefings](/en/course/conversation/) are one-page vocabulary guides for the conversations around the work. There are eight so far:

- [American politics](/en/course/conversation/american-politics/), including how to stay in the conversation without taking a side
- [Catholicism](/en/course/conversation/catholicism/) and [Protestant Christianity](/en/course/conversation/protestantism/), for the English words for things you may already know in Spanish
- [Life in the American suburbs](/en/course/conversation/american-suburbs/), which is what most pre-meeting small talk is actually about
- [Professional networking](/en/course/conversation/professional-networking/)
- [Conflict and friction at work](/en/course/conversation/workplace-conflict/)
- [Impromptu speaking](/en/course/conversation/impromptu-speaking/), for the "what do you think?" moment
- [Winning people over](/en/course/conversation/winning-people-over/), the English of rapport and influence

Each one flags the false friends, the words that look like Spanish and mean something else. *Compromise* is not *compromiso*. *Sympathetic* is not *simpático*. *Discuss* is not *discutir*. Those three alone have caused more awkward moments than any grammar mistake.

## 5. When the word is almost right

You say *depend of*. You say *explain me*. You say *make a decision* correctly and then *make a question*. Everyone understands you. Everyone also hears it.

These word partnerships are called collocations, and they are the last thing a fluent speaker fixes because nobody corrects them. [The Right Words for the Job](/en/course/verb-patterns/collocations/) is a reference and a typing drill for the combinations that business English runs on.

Then it gets specific. There is a separate set for [logistics](/en/course/verb-patterns/collocations/logistics/), [finance](/en/course/verb-patterns/collocations/finance/), [IT managers](/en/course/verb-patterns/collocations/it-managers/), [software developers](/en/course/verb-patterns/collocations/software-developers/), [doctors](/en/course/verb-patterns/collocations/doctors/), [surgeons](/en/course/verb-patterns/collocations/surgeons/) and [salon owners](/en/course/verb-patterns/collocations/salon-owners/), because the phrases a controller needs are not the phrases a developer needs.

## Where to start

Do not start at the top and work down. Start with the situation on your calendar.

- **Presenting this month?** Speak to Persuade, lessons one to three.
- **A hard conversation coming?** Disagree Without Damage, then the conflict briefing as a phrase sheet.
- **Visiting the US office?** The suburbs and networking briefings, on the plane.
- **Emails coming back with questions you already answered?** Writing Better, the lesson on starting with the point.
- **Twenty minutes and no specific pressure?** The collocations drill for your job.

One honest limit: a course can give you the phrase. It cannot tell you how you sound when you say it, or what you do when the other person does not follow the script. That takes another person in the room.
