You're in the Monday review. Someone asks what the client decided, and you open your mouth to relay the conversation you had on Friday.

*"He said me that the timeline is not realistic."*

Nobody blinks. The meaning is completely clear, the meeting moves on, and you never find out that the sentence was wrong. But a native speaker heard it — and heard it as *learner English*, not because your vocabulary is weak, but because of a two-word decision you got backwards.

Say and tell are the most common verb-pattern error I hear from Spanish-speaking professionals, and they're also the fastest one to fix permanently. There's exactly one rule.

## Why this is hard in the first place

It's not carelessness. It's structural.

Spanish uses **one verb — *decir*** — where English uses two. Look at how much work *decir* does on its own:

> *Me **dijo** que llegaría tarde.*
> *Le **dije** la verdad.*
> *¿Qué **dijo** el cliente?*

Every one of those is *decir*. In English, the first two need **tell** and the third needs **say**. Worse, the Spanish structure puts the person right before the verb (*me* dijo, *le* dije) — which is exactly the pattern **tell** uses in English, but attached to the word that translates as *say*.

So your brain does something completely logical: it takes the Spanish structure, swaps in the most common English translation of *decir*, and produces *"He said me."* The grammar is imported. The word is a coin flip. That's the whole bug.

## The one rule

Here it is, and it covers about 90% of real cases:

> **Tell needs a person. Say doesn't.**

That's it. If a person comes immediately after the verb, you must use **tell**.

| ✅ Correct | ❌ Wrong |
| --- | --- |
| She **told me** the deadline moved. | She ~~said me~~ the deadline moved. |
| I **told the client** we'd need another week. | I ~~said the client~~ we'd need another week. |
| He **said** the timeline isn't realistic. | He ~~told~~ the timeline isn't realistic. |
| What did she **say**? | What did she ~~tell~~? |

Read the two verbs as if they have different appetites. **Tell** is hungry for a person and won't work without one. **Say** refuses to touch a person directly — it wants the words themselves.

### The four patterns, side by side

Once you see the shapes laid out, the choice stops being a guess:

- **tell + person + thing** — *I told her the numbers.*
- **tell + person + that…** — *I told her that the numbers changed.*
- **say + thing** — *She said the numbers changed.*
- **say + thing + to + person** — *She said it to the whole board.*

Notice the last one. You *can* name the listener with **say** — you just have to put **to** in front of them. *"He said to me…"* is correct. *"He said me…"* never is. That little **to** is the entire difference, and it's the piece Spanish doesn't require.

## Where it actually costs you: reporting conversations

The say/tell decision shows up most in the highest-stakes thing you do in English at work — **relaying what somebody else said.** Status updates, escalations, client debriefs, passing a decision up the chain. Every one of those is reported speech.

Compare how these land:

> ❌ *"The client said me they're not happy with the delivery date."*
> ✅ *"The client told me they're not happy with the delivery date."*

> ❌ *"I told that we can move the scope."*
> ✅ *"I told them we can move the scope."* — or — ✅ *"I said we can move the scope."*

The second version in each pair doesn't just sound more correct. It sounds like someone whose account of the conversation you'd trust. When you're the person reporting what the client decided, fluency in *that specific structure* is doing quiet work on your credibility.

One more distinction worth having:

- **"He told me the deal is off."** → focus on *you receiving the information*.
- **"He said the deal is off."** → focus on *the statement itself*, listener not important.
- **"What exactly did he say to the client?"** → focus on *the precise wording*.

Native speakers pick between these without thinking. You can learn to pick between them deliberately, which is nearly as good and often more precise.

## The exceptions you memorize, not reason out

A small set of fixed expressions ignores the rule entirely. Don't fight these — they're collocations, and you learn them as whole chunks.

**Tell with no person:**

- tell the truth
- tell a lie
- tell a story
- tell a joke
- tell the time
- tell the difference

**Say with no person:**

- say hello / say goodbye
- say yes / say no
- say sorry
- say a few words
- say a prayer

This is why *"say the truth"* and *"tell hello"* both sound so wrong — the rule bends, and only in these specific slots. There are maybe fifteen of these in total. Read the list twice and you have them.

## While we're here: speak and talk

Say and tell have two cousins that Spanish also collapses (*hablar* covers both), and the same confusion follows:

- **speak** — more formal, often one direction. *May I speak to the manager?* / *She speaks four languages.*
- **talk** — conversational, two directions. *Can we talk about the budget?* / *I talked to him yesterday.*

Both take **to** before a person — *speak to him*, *talk to her* — never a person directly. So *"I want to speak you"* has exactly the same shape of error as *"He said me."* Same imported pattern, different verb.

## Test yourself in thirty seconds

Fix each one. Answers directly below — don't scroll until you've committed.

1. *She said me the report was late.*
2. *I need to tell to my manager about the delay.*
3. *He told that the client approved it.*
4. *Can you say me what happened in the call?*
5. *I want to speak with you about something — can I talk you after lunch?*

**Answers:** 1. She **told me** the report was late. 2. I need to **tell my manager** about the delay (no *to*). 3. He **told us / told me** that the client approved it — or **He said** that the client approved it. 4. Can you **tell me** what happened in the call? 5. …can I **talk to you** after lunch?

If you got 3 correct or fewer, this pattern is still costing you in meetings — and it's worth twenty focused minutes.

## Where this fits in the bigger problem

Say and tell aren't really a vocabulary issue. They're one instance of a much larger pattern: **English verbs each demand a specific structure after them, and you can't predict that structure from the meaning.**

You *tell* someone something, but you *say* something *to* someone. You *explain* something *to* someone — never *"explain me the process."* You *ask* someone *to do* something, but you *make* someone *do* it. Same idea each time, completely different shapes.
