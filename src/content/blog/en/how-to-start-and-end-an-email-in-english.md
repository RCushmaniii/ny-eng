You've written the email. The middle is fine — the numbers are right, the request is clear.

Then you go back to the top and stare at the first line. *Dear Sir? Hi? I hope this email finds you well?* And at the bottom: *Best regards? Regards? I remain attentive?*

Here's the thing: those two lines carry more weight than the paragraph between them. The first line tells the reader whether you are a peer or a supplicant. The last line tells them what happens next — or leaves them guessing. In Spanish you set both without thinking. In English, most people reach for a translation of the Spanish habit, and that is exactly where the email starts to sound like a second language.

This is the reference I give my clients. Copy what you need.

**Quick answer:** start with a greeting and the person's name, then put the reason for the email in the very first sentence. End with one line that names the next step, then a short sign-off. Everything below is the detail.

---

## How do you start an email in English?

Start with a greeting, the person's name and a comma — then go straight to the point. In US business email the default greeting is **"Hi"** plus a first name. Save "Dear" for formal situations.

| Situation | Greeting | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Colleagues, repeat clients, most business email | <span class="speak-en">Hi Maria,</span> | The safe default in the US. Not too casual. |
| First contact, neutral | <span class="speak-en">Hello Mr. Davis,</span> | A step more careful than "Hi". |
| Formal first contact with a senior external person | <span class="speak-en">Dear Ms. López,</span> | Also for complaints, legal, HR and cover letters. |
| A group | <span class="speak-en">Hi all,</span> / <span class="speak-en">Hi team,</span> | "Hi everyone," works too. |
| Two people | <span class="speak-en">Hi Maria and David,</span> | Name both. Don't write "Hi guys" to a client. |
| You don't know the name | <span class="speak-en">Dear Hiring Team,</span> | Better than "Dear Sir or Madam," which reads dated. |

**Which name do you use?** Mirror their signature. If they sign "David," write "Hi David," — even if he's a vice president. Continuing with "Mr. Davis" after he has signed with his first name creates distance he didn't ask for.

**Small crime:** "Hi Mr. Davis." It's not wrong, but it mixes a casual greeting with a formal title. Pick one register: "Hi David," or "Dear Mr. Davis,".

## What should the first line of an email say?

The first sentence after the greeting should state why you are writing. Senior readers decide in one line whether to read now or later — give them the reason before anything else.

| Situation | Opening line |
|---|---|
| First contact | <span class="speak-en">My name is Ana Ruiz, and I lead procurement at Grupo Norte. I'm writing about your packaging line.</span> |
| Introduction by a contact | <span class="speak-en">Laura Méndez suggested I get in touch with you about the Monterrey expansion.</span> |
| Replying | <span class="speak-en">Thanks for the quick reply.</span> |
| Sending something | <span class="speak-en">Attached is the Q3 report.</span> |
| After a meeting | <span class="speak-en">Following up on Tuesday's call — here are the three points we agreed on.</span> |
| Making a request | <span class="speak-en">Could you send me the signed contract by Thursday?</span> |
| Bad news | <span class="speak-en">I have an update on the shipment, and it isn't the one we wanted.</span> |
| Fixing a mistake | <span class="speak-en">You're right — the invoice was wrong. The corrected version is attached.</span> |

Notice the shift — none of these narrates. Compare <span class="speak-en">I wanted to reach out to let you know that I am sending the report</span> with <span class="speak-en">Attached is the report.</span> Same facts. One of them sounds like it was written by the person who owns the report.

### Is "I hope this email finds you well" okay?

It isn't wrong. It is filler. It's acceptable once, in a first email to someone you've never met. In an ongoing thread it delays your point, and readers skim past it.

If you want warmth, make it specific: <span class="speak-en">Congratulations on the new plant in Querétaro.</span> A specific line proves you know who you're writing to. A generic one proves you know a template.

## How do you end an email in English?

End in two parts: a **closing line** that names the next step, then a **sign-off** and your name. The closing line does the real work. The sign-off is a courtesy.

### Closing lines

| Purpose | Closing line |
|---|---|
| You need an action | <span class="speak-en">Could you confirm the revised dates by Thursday?</span> |
| You own the next step | <span class="speak-en">I'll send the updated proposal by Friday.</span> |
| You're open to questions | <span class="speak-en">Let me know if you have any questions.</span> |
| Offering a call | <span class="speak-en">Happy to jump on a call if that's easier.</span> |
| Inviting their view | <span class="speak-en">Looking forward to your thoughts.</span> |
| Thanking for help already given | <span class="speak-en">Thanks again for your help with this.</span> |

### Sign-offs, from most formal to most casual

| Sign-off | When to use it |
|---|---|
| <span class="speak-en">Sincerely,</span> | Letters, cover letters, formal complaints. Rare in everyday email. |
| <span class="speak-en">Kind regards,</span> | Formal and warm. Common in UK and international business. |
| <span class="speak-en">Best regards,</span> | The safest all-purpose business sign-off. |
| <span class="speak-en">Best,</span> | The everyday US default with colleagues and repeat clients. |
| <span class="speak-en">Thanks,</span> / <span class="speak-en">Thank you,</span> | When you've asked for something. |
| <span class="speak-en">Regards,</span> | Correct, but can read cool or curt. |
| <span class="speak-en">Cheers,</span> | Casual and British-flavored. Only if that's the relationship. |

If you're unsure, use **"Best regards,"** — it's never wrong in business.

## The translated habits that give you away

These are the patterns I correct most often with Spanish-speaking professionals. Each one is a direct translation of a perfectly good Spanish habit — which is why they're so hard to see on your own.

| Translated from Spanish | Why it sounds off | Write instead |
|---|---|---|
| <span class="speak-en">I remain attentive.</span> / <span class="speak-en">I remain at your orders.</span> (*Quedo atento / Quedo a sus órdenes*) | No native speaker writes this. It's the clearest "translated" signal in business email. | <span class="speak-en">Let me know if you have any questions.</span> — or a specific next step. |
| <span class="speak-en">Greetings,</span> as a sign-off (*Saludos*) | In English, "Greetings" opens a message, and even then sounds stiff. | <span class="speak-en">Best,</span> or <span class="speak-en">Best regards,</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">Dear Sir,</span> to someone you know (*Estimado*) | "Estimado" is normal everyday business Spanish. "Dear" in English is not — it reads like a letter. | <span class="speak-en">Hi David,</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">Cordially,</span> (*Cordialmente*) | It exists, but it's rare in US email and sounds legal. | <span class="speak-en">Best regards,</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">Sorry for the inconvenience.</span> on routine requests | Apologizing for a normal request lowers your status before you've made it. | <span class="speak-en">Thanks for your help with this.</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">I send you the report.</span> (*Le envío el reporte*) | The present tense here is Spanish structure, not English. | <span class="speak-en">Attached is the report.</span> / <span class="speak-en">I've attached the report.</span> |

**Big crime:** "I remain attentive." It's grammatically fine and it still tells the reader, instantly, that the email was composed in Spanish. One sentence undoes a well-written page.

For the phrases that do the same damage in the middle of the email, see [7 Email Phrases That Make You Sound Junior](/en/blog/email-phrases-that-make-you-sound-junior/).

## What about the subject line?

Write the subject line as a short label that tells the reader what the email is and what you need. Specific beats polite.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| <span class="speak-en">Question</span> | <span class="speak-en">Question on the March delivery dates</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">Report</span> | <span class="speak-en">Q3 sales report — for review by Friday</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">Meeting</span> | <span class="speak-en">Moving Thursday's call to 3 p.m.?</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">Urgent!!!</span> | <span class="speak-en">Decision needed today: supplier contract</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">Hello</span> | <span class="speak-en">Introduction: Ana Ruiz, Grupo Norte procurement</span> |

## One email, before and after

Here is a real-world pattern, rewritten. Same facts in both.

**Before:**

> <span class="speak-en">Dear Mr. Davis,</span>
>
> <span class="speak-en">I hope this email finds you well. I wanted to reach out to let you know that I send you the updated proposal. Sorry for the inconvenience of the delay.</span>
>
> <span class="speak-en">I remain attentive to your comments.</span>
>
> <span class="speak-en">Greetings,</span>
> <span class="speak-en">Ana</span>

**After:**

> <span class="speak-en">Hi David,</span>
>
> <span class="speak-en">Attached is the updated proposal. The main change is the delivery schedule on page 3, which now starts in March.</span>
>
> <span class="speak-en">Could you confirm by Thursday whether the new dates work for your team?</span>
>
> <span class="speak-en">Best regards,</span>
> <span class="speak-en">Ana</span>

The first version is polite. The second sounds like the person in charge of the proposal. Same length, roughly. Completely different perception.

## Checklist before you hit send

1. **Greeting matches the relationship.** "Hi" + first name unless this is a formal first contact.
2. **First sentence states the reason.** No narration, no warm-up.
3. **Last line names the next step** — who does what, by when.
4. **Sign-off is short.** "Best regards," when in doubt.
5. **No translated habits.** Scan for *attentive*, *Greetings*, *Cordially* and unnecessary *sorry*.
6. **Subject line says what the email is.** Someone should know what's inside without opening it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do you start a professional email in English?

Use a greeting with the person's name and a comma — "Hi Maria," for most business email, "Dear Ms. López," for a formal first contact — then state the reason for the email in the first sentence.

### Is "Dear" too formal for an email in English?

In US business email, usually yes. Use it for a first contact with a senior external person, formal complaints, legal or HR matters, and cover letters. For colleagues and repeat clients, use "Hi" plus their first name.

### How do you end a formal email in English?

End with a closing line that states the next step, then a sign-off and your name: "Could you confirm the revised dates by Thursday?" then "Best regards," and your name.

### What is the English equivalent of "quedo atento" or "quedo a sus órdenes"?

There is no literal one, and "I remain attentive" sounds translated. Use "Let me know if you have any questions," "Happy to help with anything else," or a specific next step.

### Is "I hope this email finds you well" professional?

It's acceptable once, in a first email to someone you don't know. In an ongoing thread it's filler. A specific warm line, or no warm-up at all, reads stronger.
