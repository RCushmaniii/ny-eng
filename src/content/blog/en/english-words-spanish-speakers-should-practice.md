Some English words are difficult because they use sounds that **do not exist in Spanish**.

Your mouth has never made the movement. Your ear has never had to hear the difference. So your brain does the sensible thing and substitutes the closest Spanish sound it knows.

That substitution is what creates the misunderstanding — not your grammar, and not your vocabulary.

But here's the part that matters: the most useful words to practice are not the exotic ones. They're the ones you **already use every day** — in your status updates, your calls, your emails, your meetings.

This page is a drill, not a lecture. **Click the speaker icon next to any English word to hear it.** Then say it out loud. Then say it again.

## 👅 The TH Sound

**TH** is the single biggest pronunciation challenge for Spanish speakers — and English has **two** of them.

Put your hand on your throat. Say <span class="speak-en">think</span>. Now say <span class="speak-en">this</span>. The second one vibrates. The first one doesn't.

Both need your tongue **between your teeth**. If your tongue stays behind your teeth, you'll produce T, D, F, or S instead — and *think* becomes "sink," *three* becomes "tree," *mother* becomes "muder."

## 🔥 Thursday = jueves

This word deserves its own section, because it stacks two difficult sounds back to back:

**TH + R**

Say it slowly. <span class="speak-en">Thursday</span>. Tongue between the teeth for the TH, then immediately pull the tongue **back and up without touching anything** for the R.

Most Spanish speakers roll or tap the R here. That single tap is what turns *Thursday* into a word your colleague has to guess at — usually on a call where you're proposing a meeting date.

Practice the whole phrase, not the word alone:

<span class="speak-en">See you on Thursday.</span>
Nos vemos el jueves.

<span class="speak-en">Can we move it to Thursday morning?</span>
¿Podemos moverlo al jueves por la mañana?

## 🔢 Thirteen vs. Thirty

This is not only a pronunciation problem. **It is a real communication problem.**

The difference is not the sound. It's the **stress** — and English speakers hear stress before they hear anything else.

| -TEEN — stress at the END | -TY — stress at the FRONT |
| --- | --- |
| <span class="speak-en">thirteen</span> | <span class="speak-en">thirty</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">fourteen</span> | <span class="speak-en">forty</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">fifteen</span> | <span class="speak-en">fifty</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">sixteen</span> | <span class="speak-en">sixty</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">seventeen</span> | <span class="speak-en">seventy</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">eighteen</span> | <span class="speak-en">eighty</span> |
| <span class="speak-en">nineteen</span> | <span class="speak-en">ninety</span> |

Now say each pair out loud, exaggerating the stress:

thir**TEEN** — **THIR**ty · four**TEEN** — **FOR**ty · fif**TEEN** — **FIF**ty · six**TEEN** — **SIX**ty · seven**TEEN** — **SEV**enty · eigh**TEEN** — **EIGH**ty · nine**TEEN** — **NINE**ty

There's a second clue, and it's easier to control than stress: the **-TEEN** words end in a hard **N**. The **-TY** words end in a soft vowel. Land on that final N and the ambiguity disappears.

If someone says <span class="speak-en">fifteen</span> when they mean <span class="speak-en">fifty</span>, the meaning changes completely:

- Prices.
- Dates.
- Ages.
- Phone numbers.
- Meeting times.
- Headcount.
- Delivery windows.

Pronunciation matters. Not for elegance — for **accuracy**.

## 👄 The English R

The English R is **very different** from the Spanish R. There is no tap. There is no roll. Your tongue never touches the roof of your mouth.

Pull the tongue back, curl it slightly, and let it float. The sound is closer to a low growl than to the Spanish *erre*.

## 💪 strong → strength → strengthen

This family is excellent pronunciation practice because it forces you through a three-consonant opening (**STR**) and then a TH ending, in the same word.

| ENGLISH | ESPAÑOL |
| --- | --- |
| <span class="speak-en">strong</span> | fuerte |
| <span class="speak-en">stronger</span> | más fuerte |
| <span class="speak-en">strongest</span> | el más fuerte |
| <span class="speak-en">strength</span> | fuerza / fortaleza |
| <span class="speak-en">strengths</span> | fortalezas |
| <span class="speak-en">strengthen</span> | fortalecer |
| <span class="speak-en">strengthens</span> | fortalece |
| <span class="speak-en">strengthened</span> | fortaleció / fortalecido |
| <span class="speak-en">strengthening</span> | fortaleciendo |

Note that Spanish has no word beginning with **st-** — which is why *strong* wants to become "estrong." There is no E at the front. Start on the S.

Now use them in sentences you'd actually say at work:

<span class="speak-en">We need to strengthen the team.</span>
Necesitamos fortalecer al equipo.

<span class="speak-en">The company strengthened its position.</span>
La empresa fortaleció su posición.

<span class="speak-en">We are strengthening our relationship with the customer.</span>
Estamos fortaleciendo nuestra relación con el cliente.

## ⏪ Difficult Past-Tense Endings

These words are common and worth drilling.

| ENGLISH | ESPAÑOL |
| --- | --- |
| <span class="speak-en">worked</span> | trabajó / trabajé |
| <span class="speak-en">asked</span> | preguntó / pidió |
| <span class="speak-en">helped</span> | ayudó |
| <span class="speak-en">stopped</span> | se detuvo / paró |
| <span class="speak-en">talked</span> | habló |
| <span class="speak-en">looked</span> | miró |
| <span class="speak-en">finished</span> | terminó |
| <span class="speak-en">changed</span> | cambió |
| <span class="speak-en">managed</span> | gestionó / logró |
| <span class="speak-en">developed</span> | desarrolló |
| <span class="speak-en">improved</span> | mejoró |
| <span class="speak-en">increased</span> | aumentó |

**Be careful with -ED. It is almost never an extra syllable.**

Here is the whole rule, and it has only three cases:

1. **-ED sounds like T** after a voiceless sound — <span class="speak-en">worked</span>, <span class="speak-en">asked</span>, <span class="speak-en">helped</span>, <span class="speak-en">stopped</span>, <span class="speak-en">talked</span>, <span class="speak-en">looked</span>, <span class="speak-en">finished</span>, <span class="speak-en">developed</span>, <span class="speak-en">increased</span>.
2. **-ED sounds like D** after a voiced sound — <span class="speak-en">changed</span>, <span class="speak-en">managed</span>, <span class="speak-en">improved</span>.
3. **-ED becomes its own syllable ONLY after a T or D sound** — <span class="speak-en">wanted</span>, <span class="speak-en">needed</span>, <span class="speak-en">decided</span>, <span class="speak-en">started</span>.

Look again at the table above: **not one of those twelve words adds a syllable.** <span class="speak-en">worked</span> is one syllable, exactly like *work* with a T on the end. If you're saying "work-ed," you're adding a beat that native speakers don't hear as past tense — they hear it as hesitation.

## 🤯 Don't Trust the Spelling

English spelling is a historical accident. It records how words were pronounced centuries ago, in several different languages. The letters are not a reliable guide.

| ENGLISH | ESPAÑOL | SAY IT LIKE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span class="speak-en">would</span> | auxiliar condicional | WUD — silent L |
| <span class="speak-en">could</span> | podría / podía | KUD — silent L |
| <span class="speak-en">should</span> | debería | SHUD — silent L |
| <span class="speak-en">though</span> | aunque | THOH — voiced TH |
| <span class="speak-en">through</span> | a través de / por | THROO |
| <span class="speak-en">thought</span> | pensamiento / pensé | THAWT |
| <span class="speak-en">comfortable</span> | cómodo | KUMF-ter-bul — 3 syllables |
| <span class="speak-en">vegetable</span> | verdura / vegetal | VEJ-tuh-bul — 3 syllables |
| <span class="speak-en">interesting</span> | interesante | IN-truh-sting — 3 syllables |
| <span class="speak-en">probably</span> | probablemente | PROB-lee |
| <span class="speak-en">business</span> | negocio | BIZ-nis — 2 syllables |
| <span class="speak-en">Wednesday</span> | miércoles | WENZ-day — 2 syllables |
| <span class="speak-en">February</span> | febrero | FEB-yoo-air-ee |
| <span class="speak-en">Thursday</span> | jueves | THURZ-day |

The pattern to notice: English **deletes syllables that the spelling promises**. *Comfortable* has four written syllables and three spoken ones. *Business* has three written and two spoken. *Wednesday* has three written and two spoken.

When you pronounce every written syllable, you sound careful — but you also sound like you learned the word from a page instead of from a room.

## Common Questions

**Why is the TH sound so difficult for Spanish speakers?**
The sound does not exist in Mexican Spanish, so the muscle movement is unfamiliar. English also has *two* TH sounds — voiceless in <span class="speak-en">think</span>, voiced in <span class="speak-en">this</span>. Most speakers substitute T, D, F, or S, and that substitution is what creates the misunderstanding.

**What is the difference between thirteen and thirty?**
The stress. <span class="speak-en">thirteen</span> is stressed on the second syllable and ends in a hard N. <span class="speak-en">thirty</span> is stressed on the first syllable and ends in a soft vowel. Stress the wrong syllable and a price, an age, or a meeting time becomes a different number.

**Does -ED add an extra syllable?**
Usually not. In <span class="speak-en">worked</span>, <span class="speak-en">asked</span>, and <span class="speak-en">helped</span> it's a single T sound. It only becomes its own syllable after a T or D sound — <span class="speak-en">wanted</span>, <span class="speak-en">needed</span>, <span class="speak-en">decided</span>.

**Do I need to eliminate my accent?**
No. See below.

## The Goal Is Not to Sound American

**Good pronunciation does not mean eliminating your accent.**

Una buena pronunciación no significa eliminar su acento.

Your accent is part of your professional identity. It says you operate in more than one language — which, in a cross-border business, is a credential and not a liability.

The goal is much simpler and much more useful:

**Be understood quickly, easily, and correctly.**

Que lo entiendan rápida, fácil y correctamente.

That's it. Not perfection. Not accent elimination. Just removing the specific sounds that force your listener to stop and decode — because every time they stop to decode, they stop listening to your idea.

Pick three words from this page. Not thirty. Three. Drill them until they're automatic, then come back for three more.
