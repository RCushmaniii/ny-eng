If you're interviewing for a sales position in English, these are the words you should be able to say **confidently and naturally**.

They aren't random difficult words. They're the words you may actually need in the room — describing your experience, your numbers, your customers, and your strengths.

Here's the thing about a sales interview specifically: you're not only being evaluated on what you've sold. You're being evaluated on **how you sell**. The interview *is* the work sample. Every hesitation over a word is data the interviewer is collecting about how you'd sound in front of their customer.

That's the good news, though — because the vocabulary is predictable. You know roughly which words are coming.

## ⚠️ Three Words Where the Wrong Sound Changes the Meaning

Most pronunciation slips just sound like an accent. These three don't — they change what the interviewer actually hears.

### 1. lead = "leed," never "led"

A sales <span class="speak-en">lead</span> rhymes with *need*. It is **never** pronounced "led."

"Led" is the past tense of the verb *to lead*, and *lead* said that way is the metal. So if you say "I had thirty leds last month," your interviewer doesn't hear a number — they hear a grammar mistake in the most basic noun of your profession.

<span class="speak-en">I generated thirty new leads last quarter.</span>
Generé treinta prospectos nuevos el trimestre pasado.

### 2. close = Z when you're closing a deal

The verb takes a **Z** sound and rhymes with *nose*. The adjective meaning "near" takes an **S** sound and rhymes with *dose*.

In a sales interview, you nearly always need the Z:

<span class="speak-en">I closed the account in March.</span>
Cerré la cuenta en marzo.

<span class="speak-en">closing the sale</span>
el cierre de la venta

Say it with an S and "I closed the deal" drifts toward sounding like "I was close to the deal" — which is the opposite of the point you are making.

### 3. career is not "carrier"

<span class="speak-en">career</span> is **kuh-REER**, stress on the second syllable, rhyming with *near*.

<span class="speak-en">carrier</span> is **KA-ree-er**, stress on the first — and it means a freight company or a phone network.

Spanish *carrera* pulls you straight toward the wrong one. This appears in the first thirty seconds of most interviews, when you describe your career, so it's worth ten repetitions.

## 🎚️ The Cognate Stress Trap

Here's the pattern that catches experienced speakers, and it's specific to sales vocabulary: **almost all of it is Latin cognates**, which feels like an advantage right up until the stress moves.

English tends to pull the stress **toward the front**. Spanish keeps it near the end. Same root, different beat — and stress is the first thing an English speaker's ear registers.

| ENGLISH — say it like this | ESPAÑOL — where the stress sits |
| --- | --- |
| <span class="speak-en">revenue</span> — REV-uh-noo | ingresos |
| <span class="speak-en">industry</span> — IN-duh-stree | in-dus-TRIA |
| <span class="speak-en">strategy</span> — STRAT-uh-jee | es-tra-TE-gia |
| <span class="speak-en">territory</span> — TERR-uh-tor-ee | te-rri-TO-rio |
| <span class="speak-en">opportunity</span> — op-or-TU-ni-tee | o-por-tu-ni-DAD |
| <span class="speak-en">percentage</span> — per-SEN-tij | por-cen-TA-je |
| <span class="speak-en">successful</span> — suk-SESS-ful | e-xi-TO-so |
| <span class="speak-en">competitive</span> — kum-PET-uh-tiv | com-pe-ti-TI-vo |
| <span class="speak-en">negotiate</span> — nuh-GOH-shee-ate | ne-go-ci-AR |
| <span class="speak-en">responsibilities</span> — ris-pon-suh-BIL-uh-teez | res-pon-sa-bi-li-DA-des |
| <span class="speak-en">profitability</span> — prof-it-uh-BIL-uh-tee | ren-ta-bi-li-DAD |
| <span class="speak-en">experience</span> — ik-SPEER-ee-uns | ex-pe-RIEN-cia |

Two extra notes on that list. **<span class="speak-en">strategy</span>** and **<span class="speak-en">strong</span>** start on the S — there is no E in front, so resist "estrategy." And **<span class="speak-en">revenue</span>** is the single most common one to get wrong in a sales interview, because you will say it more than any other word on this page.

## ⏪ Your Past-Tense Verbs — Which Ones Add a Syllable?

You will spend most of a sales interview in the past tense. The **-ED** ending splits into two groups, and mixing them up is the most audible small error in an interview answer.

**These add a whole syllable** — because the base word already ends in a T or D sound:

| ENGLISH | SYLLABLES | ESPAÑOL |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span class="speak-en">exceeded</span> | ik-SEE-did (3) | superé |
| <span class="speak-en">started</span> | STAR-tid (2) | empecé |
| <span class="speak-en">supported</span> | suh-POR-tid (3) | apoyé |
| <span class="speak-en">promoted</span> | pruh-MOH-tid (3) | ascendido |
| <span class="speak-en">generated</span> | JEN-uh-ray-tid (4) | generé |
| <span class="speak-en">negotiated</span> | nuh-GOH-shee-ay-tid (5) | negocié |

**These do not add a syllable.** The -ED is just a T or D sound stuck on the end:

| ENGLISH | SYLLABLES | ESPAÑOL |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span class="speak-en">worked</span> | WERKT (1) | trabajé |
| <span class="speak-en">helped</span> | HELPT (1) | ayudé |
| <span class="speak-en">closed</span> | KLOHZD (1) | cerré |
| <span class="speak-en">achieved</span> | uh-CHEEVD (2) | logré |
| <span class="speak-en">increased</span> | in-KREEST (2) | aumenté |
| <span class="speak-en">improved</span> | im-PROOVD (2) | mejoré |
| <span class="speak-en">managed</span> | MAN-ijd (2) | gestioné |
| <span class="speak-en">developed</span> | duh-VEL-upt (3) | desarrollé |
| <span class="speak-en">learned</span> | LERND (1) | aprendí |

The rule underneath both tables: **-ED becomes its own syllable only after a T or D sound.** Everywhere else it's a single consonant.

Say "I work-ed there for three years" and an interviewer doesn't hear careful English — they hear hesitation, at the exact moment you're establishing your track record.

## Common Questions

**How do you pronounce *lead* in a sales context?**
A sales <span class="speak-en">lead</span> is *leed*, rhyming with *need* — never "led." Said the wrong way, the most basic noun in your profession sounds like a grammar error.

**Is *close* pronounced with an S or a Z?**
Both, depending on meaning. The verb — to <span class="speak-en">close</span> a deal — takes a Z and rhymes with *nose*. The adjective meaning "near" takes an S. In an interview you almost always need the Z.

**Which words do Spanish speakers stress incorrectly?**
The cognates. <span class="speak-en">revenue</span> is REV-uh-noo. <span class="speak-en">industry</span> is IN-duh-stree. <span class="speak-en">strategy</span> and <span class="speak-en">territory</span> are stressed on the first syllable, while *estrategia* and *territorio* are stressed near the end.

**Does -ED add a syllable?**
Only after a T or D sound. <span class="speak-en">exceeded</span>, <span class="speak-en">started</span>, and <span class="speak-en">promoted</span> add one. <span class="speak-en">worked</span>, <span class="speak-en">closed</span>, and <span class="speak-en">achieved</span> do not.
