You're fluent. You run meetings in English, you close deals in English, you can hold your own in any conversation. And yet something small keeps happening.

You say *"I depend of my team."* You say *"Let me explain you the process."* You say *"I really enjoy to work with international clients."* Nobody stops you. Nobody corrects you. The meaning is perfectly clear. But a native speaker hears it — and in that half-second, a tiny label gets attached to you: *still learning.*

That label has nothing to do with your vocabulary. You know the words. It has everything to do with **verb patterns** — and it's the single most common reason skilled Spanish speakers plateau at "good English" instead of breaking through to "flawless English."

## What a verb pattern actually is

A verb pattern is the structure a verb demands after it. Look at how little the meaning helps you predict it:

- You **call** someone — but you **speak to** someone.
- You **depend on** something — not *depend of*.
- You **decide to do** something — but you **enjoy doing** something.
- Your boss **made you rewrite** it (no "to") — but they **asked you to rewrite** it.

Same idea, completely different structures. You can't choose the right one by translating the meaning. You have to know the verb *together with* its pattern — as a single chunk.

That's the trap. In school you learned English words. You memorized lists, you passed tests. But nobody taught you that *call* and *speak* — two verbs that mean almost the same thing — behave in opposite ways. So your brain does the only reasonable thing: it borrows the pattern from Spanish. *Depender de* → "depend of." *Explicarme el proceso* → "explain me the process." The word is right. The pattern is imported. And imported patterns are exactly what give you away.

## Why this hits intermediate *and* advanced speakers

Most grammar problems fade as you improve. Verb patterns don't — they *fossilize*. The more fluent you get, the faster you speak, and the faster you speak, the more you default to the pattern your brain already automated: the Spanish one. That's why you'll hear a C1 executive who negotiates flawlessly still say *"I suggested him to wait"* or *"the delay prevented us to finish."*

- **If you're intermediate (B1–B2)**, these errors are the ceiling between you and sounding genuinely professional. Fixing them is the highest-leverage grammar work you can do.
- **If you're advanced (B2–C1)**, you've solved the obvious stuff. What's left are the subtle patterns — *stop to do* vs *stop doing*, *remember to send* vs *remember sending*, *say* vs *tell* — the ones that quietly separate "very good" from "native-level precise."

Either way, the fix isn't more vocabulary or another grammar textbook. It's learning each verb with its pattern, and drilling it until the correct structure comes out without thinking.
