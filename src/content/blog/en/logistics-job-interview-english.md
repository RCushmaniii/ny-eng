You've moved freight across a border. You know what happens when a container sits in the yard past free time, when a carrier misses a pickup window on your busiest lane, when customs holds a shipment and the plant is running on two days of stock.

You've run that operation.

Then the interview is in English, and the version of you that shows up sounds like someone who *observed* the operation.

**"We had some problems with the shipments and I helped to fix them."**

That sentence is true. It's also the sentence of an assistant.

Here's the thing: the hiring manager isn't scoring your grammar. Nobody in freight has ever lost an offer over an article or a preposition. They're listening for something much narrower, and most candidates never realize it's what's being measured.

## Every logistics interview question is one of three questions

Read a job description for a logistics manager and you'll see twenty bullet points. Sit in the interview and there are only three questions, wearing different clothes:

1. **Can you hold the line when the operation breaks?**
2. **Do you own a number, or do you just report one?**
3. **Can you move people who don't report to you?**

"Tell me about a time a shipment was delayed" is question one. "Walk me through your OTIF" is question two. "How do you handle a difficult carrier" is question three.

Once you hear the real question underneath, you stop telling stories about what happened and start giving evidence about who you are.

## The pattern that costs candidates the offer

Non-native speakers under interview pressure default to the safest grammar they own. That's usually the past continuous and the vague plural — *we were having issues*, *there were some delays*, *they had problems with the carrier*.

It's safe. It's also invisible. Nobody is in those sentences.

Notice what's missing in that safe version: **you**. No decision, no number, no consequence. In an operations role, that absence is the whole interview.

The fix isn't more vocabulary. It's a structure you can run under pressure:

**Situation in one line → the number → what you decided → what it cost or saved.**

Four beats. Fifteen seconds. It works in any language, and it's specific enough that you can't hide inside it.

## 10 answers, before and after

Read the "before" out loud first. Hear how it floats. Then read the upgrade and hear someone who ran the lane.

---

### 1. "Tell me about a delay you managed"

**Before:** <span class="speak-en">We were having problems with a shipment and I helped to solve the situation.</span>

**Owner version:** <span class="speak-en">A customs hold put forty containers three days behind on our Laredo lane. I moved the priority SKUs to an expedited carrier and held the rest. We protected the plant and absorbed about eight thousand dollars in premium freight instead of a line stoppage.</span>

The second answer has a lane, a number, a decision, and a trade-off you chose on purpose. That's the whole job.

---

### 2. "What are you responsible for?"

**Before:** <span class="speak-en">I am responsible for the daily operations of the warehouse.</span>

**Owner version:** <span class="speak-en">I own OTIF for two distribution centers, about twelve hundred outbound shipments a month. When that number moves, it's mine.</span>

"Responsible for daily operations" describes a chair. Owning a number describes a person.

---

### 3. "How do you handle an underperforming carrier?"

**Before:** <span class="speak-en">I talk with them and try to improve the communication.</span>

**Owner version:** <span class="speak-en">I put the data in front of them first. When one carrier dropped to seventy-one percent on-time, I brought three months of tender acceptance to the review and gave them one quarter to close it. They came back to ninety-four. If they hadn't, I already had a backup on the lane.</span>

Notice the last sentence. Naming the contingency is what separates a manager from a coordinator.

---

### 4. "Why are you leaving your current company?"

**Before:** <span class="speak-en">Because I want to have new opportunities and grow professionally.</span>

**Owner version:** <span class="speak-en">I've taken our cross-border operation as far as the current network allows. I'm looking for a role where the network is bigger than the problem I've already solved.</span>

Nobody believes "grow professionally." It's the sentence everyone says, so it carries no information.

---

### 5. "Describe a mistake you made"

**Before:** <span class="speak-en">Sometimes I am too perfectionist and I work too much.</span>

**Owner version:** <span class="speak-en">I renewed a carrier contract without renegotiating the fuel surcharge and it cost us roughly forty thousand pesos a month for two quarters. Now every renewal goes through a rate review before I sign. I built the checklist myself.</span>

The fake weakness is the most transparent answer in interviewing, in any language. A real mistake with a real fix is the strongest thing you can say.

---

### 6. "How do you prioritize when everything is urgent?"

**Before:** <span class="speak-en">I try to organize my tasks and do the most important things first.</span>

**Owner version:** <span class="speak-en">I prioritize by what stops a line. A late shipment to a distributor is expensive. A late shipment to a plant running two days of stock shuts down production. Those aren't the same problem and I don't treat them the same way.</span>

You just showed the interviewer how you think, not that you own a calendar.

---

### 7. "Tell me about working with people who don't report to you"

**Before:** <span class="speak-en">I have good relationships with the other departments and we communicate well.</span>

**Owner version:** <span class="speak-en">Procurement and I disagreed on inventory levels for six months. I stopped arguing about the policy and started sending one weekly number: dwell time against carrying cost. Once they could see the trade-off in their own terms, we set the level together.</span>

"Good relationships" is unverifiable. A specific disagreement you resolved is evidence.

---

### 8. "Where do you see risk in our operation?"

**Before:** <span class="speak-en">I think there could be some risks with the transportation and the suppliers.</span>

**Owner version:** <span class="speak-en">From the outside, I'd want to understand your carrier concentration. If a single provider is moving most of one lane, that's leverage against you at renewal and a single point of failure in peak season. I'd want to see that split before I said it was a problem.</span>

This is the answer that ends interviews early, in the good way. You analyzed *their* operation and named the limits of what you can know from outside.

---

### 9. "How do you report to senior leadership?"

**Before:** <span class="speak-en">I send them a report every week with the information about the operation.</span>

**Owner version:** <span class="speak-en">Three lines. Where we are against plan, what's at risk, and what I've already done about it. If they have to ask me a follow-up question, my update wasn't finished.</span>

That last sentence is the kind of line an interviewer repeats to the hiring committee after you leave.

---

### 10. "Do you have any questions for us?"

**Before:** <span class="speak-en">No, I think you explained everything very well, thank you.</span>

**Owner version:** <span class="speak-en">Two. What does the first ninety days look like if this role goes well? And what's the constraint you most want this person to remove?</span>

Ask what the person is *for*. It's the fastest way to sound like a peer instead of a candidate.

---

## The one phrase to have ready

At some point you won't fully catch the question. Accent, speed, a phone connection, a term you've only ever read.

Candidates guess. That's the expensive mistake — you spend ninety seconds answering a question nobody asked, and the interviewer quietly concludes you don't listen.

Have this ready instead:

<span class="speak-en">Just to make sure I answer the right question — are you asking about the carrier relationship, or the cost impact?</span>

That is not weakness. Senior people ask for clarification constantly, because they'd rather be precise than fast. Using it makes you sound more senior, not less.

## Before your next interview

1. **Pick three numbers you own.** OTIF, dwell time, cost per shipment, claims ratio, whatever is genuinely yours. Say each one out loud in a full sentence.
2. **Write four stories in the four-beat structure.** Situation, number, decision, cost or saving. One delay, one conflict, one mistake, one improvement.
3. **Say them out loud, standing up.** Not in your head. The gap between the sentence you can think and the sentence you can say under pressure is the entire problem, and reading silently never closes it.
4. **Record ninety seconds** of the delay story. Play it back. Count how many times you say *we* when you mean *I*.

Point four is where most of the improvement lives. Mexican professionals are trained to give the team credit, and it's a genuinely good instinct. In an interview it erases you from your own operation. Give the team credit once, deliberately, and own the decision the rest of the time.

You already ran the lane. The English just has to say so.
