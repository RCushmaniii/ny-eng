You're giving the quarterly update. Your grammar is fine. Every sentence you say is correct:

> "Sales declined 12%. We lost two distributors. We are launching a promotion next quarter."

Three correct sentences. And across the table, your US counterpart is doing work you can't see — trying to figure out how those facts relate to each other. Did the distributors cause the decline? Is the promotion the answer to the distributors, or to something else? What are you actually telling them?

You gave them correct English. You made them assemble the argument themselves.

Here's the thing: nothing in that update would be flagged by a grammar teacher. That's exactly why nobody ever fixed it. The problem isn't your English. It's that your sentences are correct individually and disconnected collectively — and the listener feels the gap even when they can't name it.

---

## Where I ran into this

I was recently working with a student on academic writing when we hit a surprisingly important question. His sentences were grammatically correct. So why didn't the paragraph feel right?

The answer wasn't grammar. It was connection. Each sentence was fine on its own; none of them developed the sentence before it. The paragraph read like five separate statements standing in a row, waiting for the reader to figure out why they were together.

That conversation reminded me of something I see constantly with the executives I coach: exactly the same problem, at a much higher price point. A director whose English tests at C1 delivers a board update that lands like a list of facts. A founder pitches investors with flawless individual sentences and the room still asks, "so what's the connection between the churn number and the pricing change?"

Strong English isn't a collection of correct sentences. It's a **chain of connected ideas** — each sentence inheriting something from the one before it and handing something to the one after. When one link is missing, the listener has to do your reasoning for you. Senior listeners will do that work, once or twice. Then they quietly downgrade their assessment of you.

The good news: connection is more learnable than grammar. There are two frameworks, and you can start using both this week.

---

## Framework 1 — Build the argument: Claim → Evidence → Explanation → Link

Every unit of professional communication — a paragraph in a report, a turn in a meeting, an answer to a board question — can carry four parts:

1. **Claim** — the point you're making.
2. **Evidence** — the specific fact that supports it.
3. **Explanation** — why the evidence proves the claim.
4. **Link** — what this means for the larger decision.

Most professionals get the first two and skip the third. The pattern I hear constantly is *Claim → Evidence → New Topic*:

> "Our inventory strategy needs to change. Slow-moving inventory is up 18%. Also, we're renegotiating the freight contracts."

The listener is left holding the question you never answered: *why does 18% matter?* Your evidence does not speak for itself. It never does. The explanation is where your reasoning becomes visible — and your reasoning is what senior audiences are actually evaluating.

The same update with all four parts:

> **Claim:** "Our inventory strategy needs to change.
> **Evidence:** Slow-moving inventory increased 18% this quarter.
> **Explanation:** That increase is tying up working capital and reducing our ability to respond to shifts in demand.
> **Link:** So reducing obsolete inventory should be one of our operational priorities for Q4."

Same grammar level. Same facts. Completely different communication quality — because now the listener can follow your reasoning instead of reconstructing it.

Notice one more thing: the strong version never announces itself. It doesn't say "this evidence supports my point because…" It simply *does* the explaining. Narrating your own sentence structure is a student habit. Executives just make the connection.

## Why "furthermore" won't save you

When professionals feel their English sounds choppy, the first instinct is almost always the same: add connectors. *Furthermore. Moreover. In addition. Secondly.*

Connectors describe relationships. They don't create them.

> "Furthermore, we need to invest in the sales team."

The word *furthermore* promises the listener that this sentence builds on the last one. If the actual logical relationship isn't there, you've now made a promise the sentence doesn't keep — which sounds worse than no connector at all, because it sounds mechanical. It's the verbal equivalent of numbering your slides and calling it a narrative.

Compare:

> "Distribution explains most of the shortfall — but even at full coverage, our close rate is below where it needs to be, which is why the sales team investment matters now."

No *furthermore* anywhere. The connection is inside the sentence, not stapled to the front of it.

The test is simple: could you explain, out loud, why sentence two belongs after sentence one? If yes, a connector can make that relationship faster to hear. If no, the connector is decoration on a gap.

## Framework 2 — Build the flow: Look Back → Connect → Move Forward

This is the one to remember. When you move between ideas — between paragraphs in a report, between sections of a presentation, between topics in a meeting — the strongest transition does three things in one sentence:

1. **Look back** — name what you just established.
2. **Connect** — state the relationship.
3. **Move forward** — introduce what comes next.

In a spoken executive update, it sounds like this:

> **Look back:** "We stabilized production during Q2.
> **Connect:** That solved the immediate capacity problem — but it exposed another constraint.
> **Move forward:** Our next priority is supplier reliability."

The listener never has to wonder why you changed topics. You showed them the bridge as you crossed it.

There's a ladder here, and it's worth seeing all three rungs:

- **Basic:** "Secondly, we need to discuss suppliers." — Organized, but mechanical. You've announced a new point, not connected it.
- **Better:** "Beyond the production fixes, we also have a supplier issue." — Now the new idea touches the old one.
- **Strongest:** "Production is stable, but stability exposed how much we depend on two suppliers — which is the real risk going into Q4." — The relationship between the ideas *is* the sentence.

Most professional English lives on the first rung, because that's what English classes teach: *first, second, finally*. The third rung is what makes a listener feel they're hearing an argument instead of an agenda.

## Before and after

Here's a typical quarter-end update — five sentences, zero grammar mistakes:

> "Revenue declined this quarter. Our largest customer reduced its orders. Inventory increased. We have a new sales strategy. We expect improvement next quarter."

Nothing is wrong. And nothing is connected. The listener gets five facts and a homework assignment.

Now the same facts as a chain:

> "Revenue declined this quarter, primarily because our largest customer reduced its orders. Lower demand also drove inventory up, putting additional pressure on working capital. To address both problems, we've introduced a sales strategy focused on diversifying the customer base — and if execution stays on schedule, we expect performance to begin improving next quarter."

Read them back to back. The second version isn't more advanced English. The vocabulary is nearly identical. What changed is that every sentence picks something up from the sentence before it: *the decline → because of the customer → lower demand → both problems → improvement*. That's the chain. That's what "articulate" actually sounds like.

---

## The two formulas

Everything above compresses to this:

**Build your argument:**
Claim → Evidence → Explanation → Link

**Build your flow:**
Look Back → Connect → Move Forward

And one drill to make them yours. Take the last significant email or update you delivered in English. Find the place where you moved from one idea to the next, and ask: *did I state the relationship, or did I make the listener build it?* Rewrite that one transition with Look Back → Connect → Move Forward. Just one. That's how this becomes instinct — one bridge at a time, under real pressure.

Because the professionals who sound most articulate in English are almost never the ones with the biggest vocabulary. They're the ones whose listeners never have to work out how idea two relates to idea one.
